Planning a billion-euro observatory requires knowing, in advance, what it will be able to measure. Forecasts shape survey design, observation strategies, and ultimately which science questions we get to answer. Yet forecasts are only as reliable as the approximations that enter them.

Part of my work is devoted to auditing these approximations: quantifying the errors they introduce in forecasts {% cite Bellomo:2020pnw %} and the systematic biases they imprint on best-fit parameters {% cite Bernal:2020pwq %}. This "beware of commonly used approximations" program provides practical tools to decide when a simplifying assumption is harmless and when it silently corrupts the science case of an experiment.

I apply these methods to concrete forecasting problems for upcoming facilities — from the clustering of gravitational-wave sources with the SKA Observatory to the synergies between pulsar timing arrays and astrometry.
